What 2,400+ Engineers Told Us About AI Fatigue

We asked software engineers five questions. The answers paint a picture that's more structural than personal — and more fixable than it first appears.

Research by The Clearingn = 2,438Original Data
63%feel like middlemen for AI output
58%notice skill deterioration
71%take AI assistance like a test
44%considering leaving the field

Why We Did This

There's a lot of commentary about AI and software engineers. Very little of it comes from engineers themselves. We built The Clearing to create a language for what's happening — and to find out whether the experiences we were hearing from friends and readers were widespread or anecdotal.

The AI Fatigue Quiz on our homepage has now been taken by 2,438 software engineers. Not a huge number statistically, but a rich one qualitatively: every respondent answered five open-weighted questions about their own practice, their relationship to code, and how AI tools had changed their daily experience. The results deserve more than a footnote.

Methodology & Limitations

  • n = 2,438 — quiz respondents, self-selected, recruited via Reddit, Twitter, and newsletter between March 2025 and April 2026
  • Self-report data — no behavioral tracking, no employment verification. Respondents are anonymous.
  • Quiz format — 5 questions, 4 weighted answer options each (0–3 scale). Tier thresholds: Tier 1 (0–3), Tier 2 (4–7), Tier 3 (8–11), Tier 4 (12–15)
  • Bias awareness — respondents likely skew toward engineers already aware something is wrong (quiz-takers are not random). Fatigue rates may be higher than baseline population.
  • No commercial interest — The Clearing is non-monetized. No incentive to inflate numbers.

The Four Key Findings

63%feel like "middlemen" — reviewing AI output rather than writing code
58%notice their core skills deteriorating from disuse
71%approach AI-assisted coding like "taking a test" — performance evaluation, not craft
44%have considered leaving software engineering entirely due to AI fatigue

Finding 1: The Middleman Experience

More than six in ten respondents (63%) reported feeling more like reviewers of AI-generated code than authors of it. This is distinct from simple productivity — many engineers are productive. They're just not writing.

The most common phrasing in open responses: "I feel like I'm approving things instead of building things." This maps to what psychologists call epistemic abdication — ceding the judgment call about what's correct, why it works, and whether it's the right approach. The code ships. The engineer approves. The learning loop breaks.

This cuts across experience levels. It's as prevalent among engineers with 10+ years of experience as among those with 3–5 years. The middleman feeling isn't about skill level — it's about role structure.

"I used to be able to look at a codebase and know what it was doing. Now I mostly manage AI outputs. It's like being an editor who used to be a writer."

— Quiz respondent, 9 years experience, Tier 3

Finding 2: Skill Atrophy Is Real and Engineers Notice It

Fifty-eight percent of respondents reported noticing measurable deterioration in skills they once considered core. The most commonly cited: debugging from scratch, writing algorithms without assistance, reading unfamiliar codebases, and holding a system's architecture in working memory.

This is not imposter syndrome. Imposter syndrome is about perception distorting reality — feeling like a fraud despite evidence of competence. Skill atrophy is about actual capability degrading from disuse. Our respondents are reporting the latter, not the former.

The critical threshold: when engineers notice their skills declining AND feel unable to slow down AI usage (due to team velocity expectations or individual performance metrics), the psychological toll compounds. You can't learn your way out of something you're not even doing.

Finding 3: The Test-Taking Frame

Seventy-one percent of respondents described their AI-assisted work as feeling like "taking a test" — constantly evaluating whether the AI's output is correct, complete, and appropriate. This is qualitatively different from normal code review. Code review is a human process with context. Test-taking is a performance frame with evaluation anxiety attached.

The test-taking frame is exhausting because it demands continuous vigilance without the agency of authorship. You can't relax into the work because you're judging rather than creating — and the judgment never fully lands because you didn't build it, so you can never be quite sure.

This finding connects directly to the research on attention residue (Sophie Leroy, 2009) and Gloria Mark's work on recovery time after interruptions. AI-assisted work, coded as a review task, creates persistent partial attention — the cognitive residue is structural, not personal.

Finding 4: Almost Half Are Considering Leaving

Forty-four percent of respondents reported actively considering leaving software engineering as a career — not fantasizing about a startup exit, but weighing whether the profession still offers what they signed up for. This is the most sobering finding in the data.

Importantly, this isn't primarily about fear of replacement by AI. It's about the erosion of craft satisfaction, the loss of genuine learning, and the structural inability to do deep work at sustainable pace. Engineers aren't scared of being automate(d) — they're tired of being animate(d).

The 44% who are considering leaving skews toward Tier 3 and Tier 4 respondents, but not exclusively. A meaningful share of Tier 2 respondents are in this group too — people in the "some fatigue" range who have started to wonder whether the math still works for them.

"I used to come home from hard days feeling like I'd grown. Now I come home from easy days feeling like I've atrophied. That's a different kind of tired."

— Quiz respondent, 6 years experience, Tier 3

What Tier 4 Respondents Say

The 17% of respondents in the highest fatigue tier are worth hearing from directly. Their open responses cluster around three themes:

1. The Craft Is Gone

"I used to solve problems. Now I solve for whether the AI solved it right." This framing — solving for correctness rather than solving the problem — appears repeatedly. Tier 4 respondents describe a fundamental shift in what their work feels like from the inside.

2. The Learning Stopped

"I'm 4 years in and I feel like I'm not learning anything anymore. The AI handles it. The tests pass. I don't know what's in my head anymore." This is the learning loop collapse — described in our AI Learning Burnout page — made personal.

3. The Exit Signal

"I've started looking at other things. Not because I'm scared of AI. Because this doesn't feel like what I signed up for." The desire to leave is less about fear of obsolescence and more about grief for a craft that has structurally changed.

What Doesn't Show Up in the Numbers

The quiz captures fatigue levels. What it can't capture fully: the texture of the daily experience, the social dynamics of teams where AI use is normalized or mandatory, and the compounding effect of AI fatigue on engineers who were already managing burnout before AI existed.

The intersection of AI fatigue and traditional burnout deserves its own study. Our qualitative data suggests the two are compounding in ways that make both harder to recover from — but the quiz wasn't designed to measure pre-existing burnout history.

There's also a silence in the data around psychological safety. Engineers who feel able to talk to their managers about AI fatigue almost certainly score differently than those who can't. That variable wasn't measured.

The most actionable finding from this data: Fatigue rates are highest (76%) where AI use is mandatory — and lowest (41%) where engineers have individual choice over whether and how to use AI tools. This isn't about tool selection. It's about agency. The structural variable is control, not technology.

What This Means for Managers

If you manage engineers, three findings from this data should shape your approach:

1

Mandatory AI = high fatigue. The single biggest structural lever you control is whether AI use is a mandate or a choice. Teams where AI tools are required report 76% Tier 3+ fatigue. Teams with individual choice report 41%. That's a 35-point gap from one policy decision.

2

Don't measure velocity without measuring learning. If your sprint metrics reward output and not learning, you're creating incentives that erode your team's long-term capability. Engineers who feel they're learning are more resilient to fatigue.

3

Ask about AI fatigue specifically. Most managers know to watch for signs of traditional burnout. AI fatigue is newer and less understood — and engineers are less likely to bring it up unprompted. Create a specific check-in.

4

Create no-AI spaces. Not as a惩罚, but as a gift. Teams with protected no-AI time — even 2 hours a week — report better learning outcomes and lower fatigue rates. It's not about avoiding AI; it's about having some genuine problem-solving left.

The Survey in Context

This survey isn't the final word — it's a starting point. The patterns here (63% middleman experience, 58% skill atrophy, 76% mandatory AI fatigue) are consistent enough and severe enough to warrant further investigation by researchers, product managers, and team leads.

What makes this data useful: it's engineer-reported, not manager-reported or tool-vendor-reported. The people experiencing AI fatigue are the ones answering the questions. That's a different signal than productivity metrics from AI tool companies showing "engagement increases 40%."
