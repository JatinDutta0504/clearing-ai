**Body:**

Here's the trap that's hardest to see from the inside:

You're doing well. Metrics are strong. Your manager says you're crushing it. Your PR count is up, your code reviews are fast, and you've been promoted twice in three years.

And yet.

There's a quiet voice on Sunday evenings that says: "I'm not sure what I'm actually good at anymore."

The industry calls this high performance. I call it a performance trap.

---

The trap works like this:

AI tools have made you extremely efficient at shipping code that works. Tests pass. Features ship. Velocity metrics look great.

Your brain registers this as competence. You feel productive, capable, valuable.

What it doesn't register: every time AI solved a problem for you, your brain filed that problem under "solved" without also filing "would I still solve this without AI?"

Over time, you have a growing gap between your perceived competence and your unaided capability.

You feel fine. Your metrics look great. And somewhere underneath, the engineer who could build something real from scratch is getting quieter.

---

Why this is invisible:

1. The work looks identical from the outside — shipped code is shipped code
2. The industry rewards output, not capability
3. Your manager sees velocity, not growth (or decline)
4. AI tools feel like learning — but ease of processing isn't the same as depth of understanding
5. There's no word for "I ship fast but I'm not getting sharper"

---

The uncomfortable question:

If you had to do your job without AI for one week — not to prove a point, just because AI was down — what would you still be able to do?

What would you struggle with?

What would you not even know where to start on?

That gap is the performance trap. And most engineers who look like they're thriving are quietly standing in it.

---

What helps isn't doing less with AI. It's noticing.

Notice when you're accepting AI-generated solutions without understanding the mechanism.

Notice when you're moving fast but not learning anything new.

Notice when Sunday evenings bring a specific kind of unease — not about work being hard, but about work not being yours.

The engineers who figure this out don't usually do it by working harder. They do it by staying honest with themselves about what they can still do without assistance.

That's the work that actually matters.
