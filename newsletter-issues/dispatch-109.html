The Dispatch — Issue #109

The Autonomy Gap

May 31, 2026
7 min read For engineers using AI daily

There's a gap that's forming in engineering teams right now, and it doesn't show up in any metric we currently track.

It shows up in the meeting where someone's presenting a design and you realize they can defend the answer but not quite articulate the question it was answering. It shows up in the technical interview where someone passes every system design question with flying colors — and then in code review, their work consistently misses the tradeoffs that matter most.

It shows up in the architect who can produce excellent documentation for any system — but would struggle to identify which system shouldn't exist at all.

This is the autonomy gap. And it's one of the most quietly dangerous things AI is doing to engineering practice.

The Question Behind the Question

Every genuinely good engineer has, at some point, operated from a kind of interior compass. Not just "I know how to build this" but "I know why this question is worth asking." They can tell you not just the right answer but the right framing — the version of the problem that, once solved, actually solves the underlying issue rather than the surface-level one.

This takes years to develop. It comes from making mistakes, from building the wrong thing and feeling the cost of it, from sitting with ambiguity long enough that you learn to distinguish between productive uncertainty and unproductive noise.

Now AI can answer any question instantly. Which sounds like progress. And it is — for the question-answering part.

But the question-answering part was never the hard part. The hard part was developing the judgment to know which questions to invest in, which rabbit holes contain real value and which are beautifully argued distractions, which tradeoffs actually matter in your specific context versus which are theoretical concerns from someone else's blog post.

That judgment takes thousands of hours of practice. And AI doesn't build it — it bypasses it.

What Gets Lost in the Shortcut

When you use AI to solve a problem, you're getting the answer efficiently. But you're also bypassing the process that would have refined your judgment about that class of problem.

The junior engineer

Learns to use AI to solve every hard problem. In a year, very fast at producing solutions. Also: worse at the higher-order skill of determining which problems are worth solving.

The senior engineer

Uses AI to offload architectural decisions. Ships faster. Also: practicing less of the judgment that made them senior in the first place.

The speed is real. The judgment erosion is also real. They're happening simultaneously. And most teams are only measuring the speed.

It's Not About Dependency

Here's where it gets more nuanced:

It's not that AI makes you incapable. The proficiency is real — you can actually do more with AI help than you could before. The work is real. The output is real.

The problem is that the relationship between effort and judgment has changed, and we haven't found the right frame for it yet.

You can be highly capable and have your sense of which questions are worth your time slowly erode. These are not mutually exclusive. In fact, they're quietly co-occurring.

The engineers who are struggling most are often not the ones who can't ship. They're the ones who ship efficiently and feel, underneath the efficiency, a growing distance between themselves and their work — like they're piloting something from the passenger seat.

What the Gap Feels Like From the Inside

If you're in this, you might recognize some of these:

  • You're very fast at producing solutions but sometimes feel like a sophisticated editor more than a builder.
  • You can review AI output and find the flaws, but you're less confident about what the output should have been in the first place.
  • You find yourself answering "what should we do?" differently than you used to — more pragmatically, less from first principles.
  • You've noticed that your instincts about what matters aren't as sharp as they used to be, even as your output quality has stayed roughly the same.

If any of that landed — you're not broken. You're experiencing a specific erosion that has a name and a mechanism and, more importantly, a recovery path.

The Recovery Tactic for This Week: Question-First Hours

Pick one problem this week — any problem you'd normally just open a chat window for — and sit with it for 20 minutes before you use AI.

Not to solve it. To understand what question it's actually trying to answer.

  • Write down: What is the question behind this question?
  • Write down: What's the version of this problem that, if I actually solved it, would make the right things better?
  • Then — compare what you wrote to what AI would have given you. Not to judge yourself or the AI. Just to notice: is the AI answering the same question you're asking? Or is it answering a cleaner, simpler version that doesn't quite capture the complexity of your actual situation?

The gap between those two things is where judgment lives.

One Resource Worth Your Time
Donella Meadows — "Thinking in Systems" (1978)

This book has nothing to do with AI. That's exactly why it's useful right now. Meadows writes about how systems behave — the leverage points, the feedback loops, the places where small changes produce large effects. It's a framework for thinking about complex situations without oversimplifying them. In an AI environment where we're increasingly working with outputs we didn't fully generate, this kind of systems thinking is one of the clearest antidotes to the autonomy gap.
