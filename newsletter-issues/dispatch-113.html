Issue #113

The Input-to-Output Ratio

June 28, 2026

📖 6 min read 🌿 The Clearing For AI-Fatigued Engineers

There's a number that should terrify you.

It isn't tracked anywhere. No dashboard shows it. No one has ever put it in a quarterly review. But it tells you something true about what's happening to your skills every time you open a coding session.

Here's the question: for every unit of effort you put in, how much skill do you get out?

Not how much code. Not how many features. How much skill — the kind that stays with you, that compounds, that makes the next problem easier because this one was hard.

The traditional ratio was roughly 1:1. You struggled with something. You worked through it. The struggle left something behind — a model, a pattern, a felt sense of the problem space. Effort and learning moved together.

AI tooling has collapsed that ratio. Effort and output have decoupled. You can put in one unit of effort and produce ten units of code. But the learning — the skill — is not in the code. It was in the struggling.

The output went up. The input stayed flat. And the ratio that used to say something about your growth now says nothing at all.

What the Ratio Used to Mean

There was a time when working harder genuinely made you better.

This isn't nostalgia. It's arithmetic. Engineering skills are built through repeated cycles of: encounter a problem you can't immediately solve → apply effort → fail in instructive ways → adjust → succeed → build an internal model of why it worked. The struggle was not a bug. It was the delivery mechanism for the learning.

The feedback was baked into the work. You knew you'd learned something because it was suddenly easier to do the thing that had been hard. The next similar problem felt more tractable. You could hold more of the system in your head. You could make predictions. You had a model.

This is what the input-to-output ratio used to measure, even if no one called it that. Your effort was a reasonably reliable proxy for your growth.

What AI Changed

AI didn't just make work faster. It changed the relationship between work and learning.

When AI handles the hard part — the debugging, the architecture, the unfamiliar pattern — it doesn't just solve the immediate problem. It removes the struggle that would have built the model for the next one.

This is the hidden cost that productivity metrics don't see. Your velocity went up. Your skill growth did not.

The ratio inverted: output per unit of effort increased dramatically. The ratio of effort to learning collapsed toward zero. You're producing more than ever. You're learning less than ever. And the gap between those two facts is not a personal failure. It's a structural feature of AI-assisted work.

The Compounding Problem

The input-to-output ratio for skill growth compounds — and not in your favor.

When you struggle with a problem and learn from it, you don't just gain the skill for that specific task. You gain a more general pattern recognition ability. You learn what the problem space feels like. You develop intuition — the thing that lets a senior engineer look at a system and immediately know something is wrong without being able to articulate why.

That intuition is built in the struggling. Not in the solution.

AI removes the struggling. So the intuition doesn't build. And because intuition is the thing that makes the next problem easier, every AI-solved problem makes the following problem marginally harder to approach without AI. Not because you got dumber. Because the scaffolding for judgment wasn't built.

This compounds across months. The engineers who notice this most acutely are the ones who have been working this way for a year or more. The gap between what they can do with AI and what they can do without it has grown wide enough to see. And what they see worries them more than they'd admit in a sprint retrospective.

"I can build anything now. I ship more code than I ever have. But when something breaks at 2am — really breaks — I have no idea what is happening. I just prompt-storm until something sticks."

— A senior engineer, 12 years experience, speaking honestly in a 1-on-1

Why the Metrics Don't Catch It

Your velocity metrics look great. Your cycle times are down. Your PRs are closing faster. Your review throughput is up. Every dashboard green.

The metrics measure output. They don't measure input — not the real input, not the cognitive effort, not the skill-building struggle. They measure work product. And the work product is genuinely higher with AI.

This is why the problem is so hard to address from inside a team. The metrics say everything is fine. The engineers who are paying close attention say something is wrong. And without a shared vocabulary for the difference between shipping and growing, the engineers who notice the problem sound like they're complaining about free productivity.

The input-to-output ratio is personal. You know whether the work you're doing is teaching you anything. You know whether you're finishing a week and feeling like the next week will be easier because of what you learned, or whether you're finishing a week and knowing that you'll need AI just as much on Monday because nothing stuck.

That's the ratio that matters. And it's not on any dashboard.

The Metric Worth Tracking

Here's the thing worth paying attention to:

Every week, ask yourself one question:

What did I learn this week that I didn't know last week?

Not what did I ship. What did I learn.

If the answer is unclear — if you're not sure what you've gained from the week's work — that's data. It means the input-to-output ratio for that week was low. Not zero. Low. The work happened. The code shipped. But the learning that should have accompanied it didn't accumulate.

This isn't about being harder on yourself. It's about noticing what's actually happening. Because if you can't see the ratio, you can't manage it. And if you can't manage it, the default direction is toward more output and less growth — forever, not just until you notice.

State What It Looks Like The Ratio
Healthy Struggled, learned, can do it faster next time ~1:1 — effort → skill
Mild Erosion Shipped more, not sure what you learned 10:1 — effort → output, near 0 → skill
Significant Gap Can't remember the last time something was hard Output up 3x, skill flat or declining
Critical Considered leaving tech; feels hollow Maximum output, minimum growth

🔬 The Practice That Changes the Ratio

You don't have to stop using AI. That's not the point.

The point is: some fraction of your work needs to be yours.

  • Tier 4 (considering leaving the industry): Multiple hours per week of unaided practice. Real problems. The discomfort of not knowing immediately.
  • Tiers 2–3 (aware something shifted): One intentional non-AI session per week. Not to prove anything. To keep the thread alive.
  • Tier 1 (mindful use): Audit your ratio monthly. Protect the fraction that stays yours.

The input-to-output ratio isn't permanently broken. It's just defaulting in the wrong direction. The direction is a choice, not an accident — not anymore.

What 113 Issues Teaches

This is our 113th issue. We've been writing about AI fatigue every week since March of this year.

What we've learned from the engineers who write to us, take the quiz, share their stories, and send us the unfiltered account of what's happening in their teams is this: the engineers who are doing fine are the ones who are paying attention to the ratio.

They're not the ones using AI the least. They're not the most talented or the most disciplined. They're the ones who have noticed that effort and learning used to move together, and they're actively protecting that relationship now.

The engineers who are struggling are almost always the ones who noticed the ratio had shifted but didn't know what to do about it — and so they just kept working and hoping the feeling would pass.

It doesn't pass. But it can be addressed. The ratio is rebuildable.

The question each week is simply: what did you put in, and what did you get out?

That's worth tracking. Not for a retro. For yourself.
