The Compounding Deficit

July 16, 2026 · 7 min read · For engineers navigating AI fatigue

The Math Nobody Is Doing

Every time you reach for AI instead of working through something yourself, you skip a rep. The rep is the thing that builds capability. Skipping it isn’t free — it costs you a unit of the skill you would have built.

In the short run, this is invisible. You ship faster. The output looks fine. Nothing hurts.

The problem is the cumulative effect. One skipped rep is noise. Six months of skipped reps is a capability debt that’s hard to name but easy to feel. A year of it is a significant erosion.

The math: Assume 30 minutes per day of genuine struggle that would have built capability — the hard debugging, the architecture reasoning, the moment you would have pushed through and come out knowing more.

AI-assisted development for 5 hours per day, 5 days per week. That’s 2.5 hours per week of potential skill formation displaced by AI-assisted production.

Over a year: 125 hours. That’s three full work weeks of the struggling that builds an engineer.

Over three years: 375 hours. Two full quarters of the work that would have made you better.

Why the Deficit Compounds

Two things make the compounding worse than the simple math suggests:

The Capability Illusion

When AI generates good output, you tend to attribute your own capability to AI generation. Your brain conflates “code that works” with “code I can produce.” You update your self-assessment upward while your actual capability erodes. The illusion and the deficit grow in parallel.

The Reverse Transfer Problem

Research on deliberate practice (Ericsson et al., 1993) shows that capability doesn’t just stall when you stop practicing — it actively degrades without the friction that maintains it. The skill you don’t use doesn’t just sit there. It erodes.

What you’re managing isn’t a rusted skill. It’s an actively eroding one. And the erosion rate increases the less you use it.

The Deficit Audit

You can’t measure the exact erosion rate. But you can get a rough read:

Could you build your last three shipped features without AI, from scratch, in the time it took you to ship them with AI?

If the honest answer is no for more than one of them — the compounding deficit is live.

Could you explain your last five architectural decisions without a code reference, an AI tool, or a colleague walking you through it?

If not — the compounding deficit is compounding.

When was the last time you spent more than 30 minutes stuck on something without AI assistance?

If you can’t remember — or if the memory produces anxiety instead of recognition — the deficit is substantial.

The Asymmetry Worth Naming

Here’s the asymmetry that matters most: the capability you lose compounds just as fast as the capability you would have built.

If you had spent the last two years doing genuine struggling engineering, your capability would have grown. Instead, you spent two years doing AI-assisted production, and your capability eroded. The gap between where you could have been and where you are has been growing every week.

The compounding works in both directions. You’ve been running the negative compounding asset while the positive compounding asset sat idle.

What the Recovery Path Looks Like

The good news: the compounding deficit is addressable. The brain retains the capacity for the cognitive work AI displaced — what Bjork’s desirable difficulties research calls “the struggle is the learning.”

Once per week, pick one meaningful technical problem and solve it with zero AI. Not for production. Not for speed. For practice. The 30 minutes you spend wrestling with it is the rep that rebuilds the capability that AI skips.

The compounding deficit takes time to show up and time to address. You’ve been running the negative compounding for over a year. The positive compounding takes at least as long.

Start now. The math is more urgent than it looks.
