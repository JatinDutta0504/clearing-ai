# The Dispatch #85 — The Velocity Trap

*Issue #85 — May 25, 2026 — For engineers who are fast but not sure they're getting better*

---

## The Velocity Trap

Your PR count went up 40% last quarter.

Your debugging speed didn't change.
Your architecture judgment didn't improve.
Your ability to reason about a system without AI has probably gotten worse.

Most people would call that a win. I think it's the most important data point you're not tracking.

Here's what I mean.

---

## Two Numbers You Should Know

There's shipping velocity — how fast you ship features, close tickets, merge PRs.

And then there's capability velocity — how fast your actual skills are growing. Your unaided ability to solve problems, debug systems, architect solutions, reason about tradeoffs.

These used to move together. Ship more, learn more. The work was the training.

AI separated them. You can now move fast on shipping velocity while capability velocity stays flat or declines. The two numbers that used to be yoked together are now decoupled — and most engineers are only tracking the one that looks good in the quarterly review.

The gap between those two numbers is where AI fatigue lives.

---

## Why You Don't Feel It

Here's the part nobody talks about: you don't feel capability velocity decline in real time.

When AI generates code that works, your brain registers success. The tests pass. The feature ships. The loop closes cleanly and quickly. It feels like progress.

What it doesn't feel like is learning — because the learning used to come from the struggle, and the struggle is gone. The AI took the hill so you could drive through to the next checkpoint. You arrived. But you didn't climb.

Over months, this creates a quiet divergence. You feel fine. Your metrics look good. And somewhere underneath, the engineer you were two years ago — the one who could sit down with a blank file and build something real — is getting smaller.

The first time you really notice is usually when AI is unavailable. An outage. A new codebase. A context where you have to do it yourself. And the silence in your own head is louder than you expected.

---

## The Measurement Nobody's Talking About

The engineers who figured this out early have started tracking something I haven't seen anyone else talk about: confidence unaided.

Not "can you ship it with AI?" — that's a solved problem.

"Can you ship it without AI?"

That's the number that tells you whether you're growing or just accelerating. Whether the tools are extending you or replacing you. Whether you're still building the engineer you want to become, or just maintaining the performance of the engineer you appear to be.

If you've never measured this, here's a simple test: one afternoon, pick a problem you've already solved with AI. Close the AI tab. Try to solve it yourself.

Don't do it to prove a point. Do it to calibrate.

---

## The Question Worth Sitting With

I'm not going to tell you to stop using AI tools. That would be like telling you to stop using a calculator and do long division by hand. The efficiency is real. The problem isn't the tools — it's that we lost the feedback loop.

The question worth sitting with isn't "should I use AI or not?"

It's: "What do I want to still be able to do without AI in two years?"

That's the skills question. That's the calibration question. And it's the question most engineers haven't explicitly answered — which means they're building toward nothing in particular, just throughput.

---

## What Actually Helps

One practice that's been most useful for engineers who found their way back: the weekly unaided session.

One hour per week. Work on something real — not a toy problem, something you actually care about — without AI. Not to suffer. Not to prove independence. Just to stay in touch with what you can still do on your own. To keep the calibration fresh.

The goal isn't to go without AI forever. The goal is to stay sharp enough that when you do use AI, it's a choice — not a dependency.

---

## What Nobody Tells You

Here's what I don't say enough: this isn't your fault.

The industry created the conditions for this. The velocity demands, the FOMO, the mandatory tool mandates, the hiring bar that now implicitly includes "AI proficient." You didn't choose to have your learning loop disrupted. You were handed tools and told to use them and then judged on outputs that looked like growth.

The question now is whether you want to do something about the gap — not because you have to, but because you're an engineer, and engineers care about accurate models of reality.

That's what this newsletter is for. Not to make you feel bad about what you can't do. To help you stay sharp enough to keep doing the work you care about.

Go build something.
