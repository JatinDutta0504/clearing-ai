# Dispatch #95 — The Autonomy Gap

**Week of June 22, 2026**
**Theme: Why having answers isn't the same as knowing which questions to ask — and what the gap costs you professionally and personally**

---

## The Opening

There's a moment that happens to a lot of engineers after about a year of heavy AI use.

You can build things. You can ship things. You can answer questions — not yours, but questions in general. You can debug in the sense that you paste an error into a chat and get a solution back. You can participate in code review by describing what you think the code does and having AI verify it.

But there's a specific thing that's gone quiet.

It's the thing where you'd encounter a problem and — before asking anyone, before looking anything up, before going to the docs — you'd have a feel for which direction to push first. Not certainty. Just: a direction. A guess informed by experience. A sense that this was probably a caching issue, or that the bug was in the state management, or that the performance problem was a query N+1 situation.

You used to have that. Now when you encounter a new problem, your first reflex is to ask AI. Not because you're unsure — because the reflex is faster than the feel.

And the feel is what's quietly atrophying.

---

## What the Autonomy Gap Actually Is

The autonomy gap is the widening distance between what you can do with AI assistance and what you can do on your own.

It's not just capability — it's a kind of internal compass. The sense of direction that tells you where to look before you know what you're looking for. That compass used to develop through years of working through hard problems without a safety net. Now the safety net is always there, and the compass doesn't get exercised as much.

Here's what makes it hard to notice: the gap doesn't show up in your output. You still ship. You still solve problems. You still get things working. The work looks the same from the outside. The velocity numbers are fine — often better than fine.

The gap shows up in a different way: in rooms where you're expected to have opinions, you find yourself reaching for AI to form one. In situations where you'd normally have a gut call about direction, you go quiet and think "I'll ask the AI later." In conversations about architecture, you find yourself describing what you think rather than what you know — because you're not sure anymore, and the AI's answers don't quite settle the question in your own mind.

You have answers. You don't have the thing that used to give you answers before there was an AI to ask.

---

## The Experience Nobody Tells You About

There's a specific experience that signals the autonomy gap more than anything else.

You know that feeling — the one you probably had in your first year of coding — where you'd encounter something completely new, something you had no framework for, and you'd just... sit with it. You'd turn it over. You'd read things that only partially made sense. You'd write code that didn't work and then you'd try to figure out why and then you'd try something else.

That sitting with confusion — the productive confusion, the kind that eventually resolves into understanding — is not something you can do with AI. Not because AI can't help, but because AI always offers a way out of the confusion before you've had the chance to develop the feel for the problem.

And the thing is: the feel for the problem is exactly what makes you dangerous in the way that matters. Not the ability to use AI — everyone can do that. The ability to know which questions are worth asking. The ability to sense when something is off before you can articulate why. The ability to catch the AI's confident mistake because you have enough experience with this domain to know when the answer doesn't fit.

That's not a skill you can develop with AI. It's a skill you develop by being confused without a way out for long enough that you develop your own way through.

The autonomy gap is the gap between having answers and having the internal compass that tells you which questions to ask.

---

## What It's Costing You

The autonomy gap costs more than most people admit — not because they're hiding it, but because it's hard to see the cost in the moment. The cost shows up slowly, over time, in ways that are hard to attribute to a single cause.

Here are three of the ways it shows up most often:

**In problem-solving:** You can solve problems that AI can solve. You increasingly struggle with problems that AI can't solve well — the messy ones, the ambiguous ones, the ones that require knowing which question to ask before you know the answer. And those are increasingly the problems that matter.

**In professional confidence:** There's a quiet erosion of the confidence that comes from knowing you can figure things out. Not "I can ask AI to figure it out" — but "I, personally, can work through this." That confidence used to be the foundation of how you showed up in rooms. It's thinning.

**In craft identity:** You used to be the person who could look at a system and understand it. Now you can look at a system and describe what AI says it does — but you're not sure what it actually does under the hood. The internal model is getting thinner, and with it, the sense of craft that used to be part of your identity as an engineer.

None of these are catastrophic on their own. They're invisible until they're structural.

---

## The Thing About Autonomy That Nobody Says

Here's the thing that's hard to say out loud because it sounds like you're against AI:

Autonomy isn't about refusing AI. It's about maintaining the internal capacity that AI doesn't give you — and can't give you — no matter how good it gets.

The question is not "should I use AI?" The answer to that is almost always yes. The question is: "am I maintaining the capacity to work without it when it matters?" Because there will be moments — critical moments, moments where the AI is wrong or the context is novel or the stakes are high — when the capacity to think and problem-solve independently will be exactly what's needed.

And that capacity doesn't rebuild itself. If you're not actively maintaining it, it's declining.

The autonomy gap is not about being anti-AI. It's about making sure you're still in the room as a full engineer when AI is one of the tools in the room — not a crutch that replaces the part of you that used to have direction before you had an answer.

---

## The Practice This Week

Here's the thing I'd ask you to notice this week:

**Pick one problem — just one — where you'd normally ask AI immediately. Instead, spend 15 minutes just thinking about it first.**

Not trying to solve it without AI. Not refusing to use it. Just: before you open the AI tab, see if you can say in your own words what you think the problem might be, and which direction you'd push first.

The AI will still be there when you're done. The goal isn't to prove you don't need it. The goal is to notice whether your internal compass still has a direction — and whether you want to protect that direction or let it go quiet.

The gap between having answers and knowing which questions to ask is not a skill gap. It's an autonomy gap. And it's yours to decide whether you want to keep it or trade it away for speed.
