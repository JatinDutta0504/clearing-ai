Who Are the Attention Merchants?

The phrase "attention merchants" comes from Tim Wu's 2016 book The Attention Merchants. Wu documented how media companies — newspapers, radio, television, eventually social media — learned to capture human attention and sell it to advertisers. The product wasn't the content. The product was the eyeballs. The content was the bait.

AI coding tools are the next iteration of this model, but with a twist: the advertisers have been replaced by investors, and the currency is data and engagement metrics instead of impressions.

When you use a free or subscription-based AI coding tool, you're not the customer — you're the source of three things:

  • Training data. Every prompt, every accepted suggestion, every code review you do inside the tool contributes to the model's training set. Your work — the actual intellectual labor — is being harvested to improve a product you'll continue paying for or that will be used to compete with you.
  • Engagement metrics. The time you spend in the tool, the features you use most, the patterns of your workflow — all of this is tracked to demonstrate "product-market fit" to investors. Active users are the metric. More time in the tool = more evidence of value = higher valuation = more investment.
  • Ecosystem lock-in. The more you use a tool, the more your workflows adapt to it. Switching costs increase. You become a permanent user not because the tool is the best option, but because leaving would cost too much in retraining and context-switching.

The "productivity" framing is intentional. It reframes the transaction from "we're mining your attention" to "we're empowering you to do more." The productivity narrative is the smoke. The attention extraction is the fire.

How AI Tools Mine Your Attention

Traditional attention merchants (social media, news, entertainment) captured attention through passive consumption. You scrolled. You watched. The attention was extracted while you sat relatively still.

AI tools extract attention through active production. You write code. You review suggestions. You make decisions. Each interaction is higher-stakes than scrolling, which means each interruption is more costly — and the tools have been designed to interrupt constantly.

The Notification Cascade

Most AI tools have evolved beyond "just being there when you need them." They're now designed to remind you they exist:

Common AI Tool Engagement Mechanisms

• In-editor suggestions that appear whether you asked or not (hovering, waiting for acknowledgment)

• Email/notification digests: "Here's what Claude worked on while you slept"

• Community updates: "New features in Copilot, see what's changed"

• Version updates: "Claude 4 is here, here's what's new" — not critical, but designed to trigger a check-in

• Prompt/template sharing within the tool's ecosystem: "Your colleague's prompt template was shared with you"

• Badge/score systems: "You've used Copilot for 47 consecutive days — keep the streak going"

Each of these is a re-engagement trigger. The goal is to keep you inside the tool's ecosystem at all times, not just when you have a specific task.

The Attention Residue Problem

Sophie Leroy's 2009 research on attention residue documented something critical: when you switch tasks, a piece of your attention stays behind. The unfinished task occupies working memory even after you've moved on. This is why a stopped workflow is harder to restart than a paused one — the pause leaves a thread; the stop leaves a wound.

AI tools are uniquely effective at creating attention residue because they generate constant partial tasks:

Research Finding

Leroy's "attention residue" effect is strongest when the task you left was in a cognitive state of "almost done" or "in progress." AI tools generate this state constantly — every suggestion that's partially accepted, every prompt that's waiting for a response, every context window that's been populated but not actioned. Each creates a residue that occupies working memory.

The Notification Overhead

Research from Gloria Mark at UC Irvine found that after an interruption, it takes an average of 23 minutes to return to full focus on the original task. AI tool notifications — even the subtle in-editor kind — create micro-interruptions that individually seem negligible but cumulatively destroy deep work capacity.

The math: if you receive 15 AI tool notifications per day, and each requires 3-5 minutes to fully reset focus, that's 45-75 minutes of daily focus lost to notification overhead alone — before accounting for the larger context switches when you actually engage with the notification.

The Engagement Signals You're Ignoring

Most engineers think about AI tool costs in terms of time. But the more insidious costs are behavioral — the engagement patterns that indicate your attention has been successfully captured and is being continuously extracted.

Signs You're Being Mined for Attention

Tool anxiety: You feel genuine stress when a major AI tool updates — not because your workflow is disrupted, but because you fear being left behind or missing something important.

Preference discussions: You've had passionate debates with colleagues about which AI tool is "better" — not as a productivity analysis, but as a preference/entertainment discussion, like arguing about sports teams.

After-hours checking: You check your primary AI tool outside of work hours. Not deliberately — it's just there. You're debugging something over the weekend and you want to verify a code path. The tool is right there. It's not a deliberate choice — it's muscle memory.

Habitual checking: You open the AI tool app or dashboard even when you don't have a specific task — just to see if there's anything new, any updates, any interesting prompts from the community.

Switching without trigger: You switch to an AI tool without a specific task in mind — not triggered by a notification or a work need, but by the simple impulse to check.

These are engagement patterns, not productivity patterns. The tools are designed to trigger them, and they're working exactly as intended.

The Habit Loop

B.F. Skinner's research on variable reward schedules shows that behaviors tied to unpredictable rewards are the most compulsive. AI tools are built on this principle:

Behavioral Economics

Every AI tool suggestion is a variable reward — sometimes it's brilliant, sometimes it's mediocre, sometimes it's wrong. This unpredictability is not a bug; it's the mechanism that keeps you checking. Your brain can't predict whether the next suggestion will be amazing, so it keeps you showing up to find out. This is the same mechanism that makes slot machines more addictive than vending machines.

The Actual ROI (Spoiler: It's Lower Than You Think)

Most engineers calculate AI tool ROI as:

Time saved on implementation = Value delivered

This is incomplete. The full calculation looks like this:

Cost/Benefit Category How to Measure Typical Value
Time saved on implementation Track actual vs. estimated time for AI-assisted tasks +20-40% faster
Time lost to AI context management Log every prompt, review, and revision cycle -8-15% of saved time
Attention residue costs Track focus quality via hourly self-check-ins -10-20% of deep work capacity
Skill atrophy costs Track ability to solve problems without AI (monthly) -5-15% of independent problem-solving ability
Ecosystem lock-in costs Calculate switching overhead and tool subscription costs -$50-500/month depending on tool stack
Notification overhead Track every AI-related notification and context switch -30-60 min/day in focus time

When you include all costs, the net ROI for intensive multi-tool AI usage is frequently negative — particularly for engineers who are using multiple AI platforms simultaneously and haven't calibrated their usage to their actual workflow needs.

The Tool Count Problem

The average software engineer now uses 3-4 AI tools regularly (IDE integrated AI, standalone chatbot, code review tool, documentation AI). Each tool has its own:

  • Notification system
  • Update cadence
  • Feature set and interface
  • Context requirements (what you've been working on recently)
  • Subscription cost and lock-in mechanics

Managing the cognitive overhead of a multi-tool AI stack is itself a significant cognitive task. Many engineers haven't calculated what this overhead costs them in terms of focus, decision-making capacity, and mental bandwidth.

How to Reclaim Your Attention

You don't need to abandon AI tools. You need to use them deliberately rather than habitually. The goal is to be the principal — the one who decides when and how AI is used — rather than the agent — the one who's being used by the tool.

The Attention Reclamation Protocol

1
Audit your attention. For one week, track every time you open an AI tool. Note: the time, the trigger (notification, habit, or deliberate), and how long you spend. Calculate the total. Compare to the time the tools are actually saving you.
2
Consolidate to one. Pick one AI tool as your primary. Not because it's "best" — because having one tool reduces the cognitive overhead of maintaining multiple contexts. Close the accounts you don't absolutely need.
3
Establish attention boundaries. Set specific times for AI tool use — not when you feel like it, but when you decide it's appropriate. Turn off all non-essential notifications. Make the tool opt-in, not opt-out.
4
Practice AI-free work. Designate one category of your work where AI is never used — ideally the work you find most satisfying. The ability to work without AI is a skill that degrades without practice. Protect it.
5
Opt out of the engagement layer. Avoid AI tool communities, forums, and discussion threads during non-work hours. Unfollow AI tool accounts on social media. The goal is to keep the tool from occupying your mind when you're not using it.

The Deeper Fix: Reframe the Relationship

The attention merchant model works because we've accepted the framing that AI tools are "empowering" us. The reframe is simple:

The Reframe

AI tools are contractors you hire for specific tasks. You wouldn't let a contractor send you daily update emails about their work, remind you of their existence every time they update their service, or compete with you for attention during your off hours. Treat AI tools the same way: hire them for the job, dismiss them when the job is done, and don't let them become your landlord.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are AI tool companies described as attention merchants?
AI tool companies profit from capturing and monetizing your attention — not through subscriptions (which are modest), but through the data, engagement metrics, and behavioral patterns you generate while using their tools. Every prompt, every code suggestion accepted, every context window populated is data that trains future models and demonstrates engagement to investors. The more time you spend in the tool, the more valuable the tool becomes. 'Free' AI tools are particularly effective attention merchants because the cost of entry is zero — you don't feel the transaction happening.
What is the hidden cognitive cost of AI coding tools?
The hidden cognitive cost is attention fragmentation — not just the time spent using AI tools, but the residual attention residue that persists after you close the tool. Each tool has its own notification system, update cadence, and engagement mechanism designed to pull you back. The cost isn't just the minutes spent in the tool; it's the cognitive overhead of managing multiple AI relationships, staying current with capabilities, and maintaining context across different AI platforms. Research on attention residue (Sophie Leroy, 2009) shows that unfinished tasks occupy working memory even after we've moved to other work — AI tools generate unfinished cognitive threads constantly.
How do AI tools fragment focus differently than other digital distractions?
Most digital distractions (social media, news, entertainment) fragment attention through passive consumption — you watch, you scroll, you don't produce. AI tools fragment attention through active production: you're writing code, making decisions, reviewing suggestions. This is cognitively more expensive because each context switch involves decision-making, not just passive reception. When you switch from Copilot to Slack to Claude to your IDE, you're not just changing windows — you're changing cognitive modes, which incurs a switching cost documented in cognitive psychology as the 'attentional bottleneck'. AI tools create more frequent, lower-magnitude switches than traditional multitasking, which makes them harder to perceive as disruptive.
What's the real ROI of AI coding tools when attention costs are included?
Most engineers haven't calculated the full ROI of AI coding tools. The calculation looks like this: Time saved on implementation minus time lost to AI context management (prompt writing, reviewing suggestions, managing version control with AI-generated code, debugging AI output) minus attention residue costs (the cognitive overhead that persists after AI use) minus ecosystem lock-in costs (switching overhead between tools, learning new interfaces). For many engineers using multiple AI tools intensively, the net productivity gain is substantially lower than the surface-level time savings suggest — and for some, the net is negative.
How do you know if you're being mined for attention by AI tools?
The signals: You have multiple AI tool subscriptions or accounts and use more than one regularly. You check AI tools outside of work hours. You feel genuine anxiety when an AI tool updates (changes its interface, deprecates features, or changes its model) — not because your workflow is disrupted, but because you fear missing something. You can't remember the last time you wrote a significant piece of code without using an AI tool. You've had conversations about which AI tool is 'better' with colleagues — not as a productivity discussion, but as a preference discussion, like discussing which streaming service you prefer. These are engagement patterns, not productivity patterns. They're designed to be sticky.
How do you reclaim attention from AI tool companies?
First, measure the real cost: track your attention for one week using a simple log — every time you switch to an AI tool, note the time, the trigger (notification, habit, deliberate search), and how long you spend. Calculate the total and compare to the time the tool is actually saving you. Second, establish attention boundaries: pick one AI tool as your primary (not because it's 'best', but because it reduces the cost of maintaining multiple tool contexts). Third, designate AI-free work periods — not because AI is bad, but because the ability to work without AI is a skill that degrades without practice. Fourth, opt out of the engagement layer: turn off non-essential notifications, avoid AI tool communities and discussion forums that keep the tool present in your mind during non-work hours. The goal isn't to abandon AI tools — it's to use them deliberately rather than habitually.
